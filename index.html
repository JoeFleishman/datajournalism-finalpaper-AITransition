Who Pays for the AI Transition?

By Joseph Fleishman

Anxiety about artificial intelligence is hard to escape right now. It is in the news, in classrooms, and all over social media, where every new tool seems to arrive with a promise and a warning attached. What once sounded like a distant Silicon Valley idea is now changing how people think about work, security, and the future. The harder question is which workers will be reorganized first and who will have enough power to shape the terms of that change.

Work has never been only about a paycheck. A job gives people income, but it also gives them routine, status, identity, bargaining power, and a way to imagine the future. Employment is tied to health care, housing, retirement, and social worth in ways that make job insecurity feel personal almost immediately. When people worry about automation, they are asking whether the rules that once made a stable life possible are being rewritten without them.

To move past the headlines, it helps to look at the people shaping the argument. In a recent CBS News interview, Jamie Dimon, CEO of JPMorgan Chase, described a future in which AI could mean shorter workweeks, longer lives, and major medical breakthroughs. He made a similar point three years earlier in a Bloomberg TV interview, when he said future generations might work three and a half days a week while maintaining high living standards. Dimon also told younger workers to focus on skills that machines still struggle with, including communication, teamwork, and judgment. He acknowledges disruption, and his version of the future assumes the gains can be shared widely enough to make the disruption worth it.

That kind of optimism is common among executives who see AI first as a productivity tool. From the top of a large company, the technology looks like a way to remove friction, speed up analysis, and make organizations more efficient. It also fits a familiar story: new machines arrive, old tasks disappear, new tasks emerge, and the economy finds another balance. The weaker part of the story is what happens in between, because a technology can make an economy richer while leaving many workers less secure.

Not all visions of the future come from boardrooms. On TikTok, Grimes claimed that "AI is actually the fastest path to communism," imagining algorithms that could run farming, reduce corruption, and create abundance with less human involvement. She later said the video was partly a joke, but the idea stuck because it named a real desire. If machines can do more of the work, then people naturally ask whether life could become less punishing. The fantasy sounds exaggerated, but the frustration behind it is not hard to understand.

The joke landed because many younger workers already feel that the economy has broken its promises. They came of age during rising housing costs, unstable job markets, heavy debt, and a weakening belief that education or effort will reliably lead to mobility. In that setting, a future with less tedious work and more freedom does not sound like a luxury; it sounds like a response to exhaustion. The meme worked because it made a serious grievance easy to circulate. A half-serious joke could say what a policy paper says more carefully: people want technology to give them time back, while employers often talk about efficiency first.

The same mix of promise and anxiety appears inside the AI industry itself. Dario Amodei, the co-founder and CEO of Anthropic, told Axios that AI companies and governments should stop softening the possibility of serious white-collar job disruption. That warning complicates the usual divide between tech promoters and tech critics because it comes from someone building one of the major AI systems at the center of the debate. Amodei has also written a more optimistic essay, "Machines of Loving Grace," imagining AI as a force that could improve health, science, and economic life. His two positions show how the same industry can promise abundance while warning that the transition may be brutal.

Andrew Yang brings the argument back to policy. The entrepreneur and former 2020 Democratic presidential candidate built much of his political identity around the warning that automation could displace millions of workers, especially people in routine or repetitive jobs. His 2020 presidential campaign centered on the Freedom Dividend, a universal basic income proposal meant to soften automation shocks before they spread through the economy. In a recent newsletter, he updated the same argument for the generative AI era with a shorter slogan: tax the bots. Yang's argument turns the abstract fear of displacement into a question of public finance.

If machines help companies replace or reduce human labor, Yang argues, then the tax system should capture part of that value and send it back into the economy. The idea is controversial, but it asks a concrete question: if automation creates wealth while weakening the link between work and income, who should pay for the transition?

Yarvin approaches the same question of power from another direction. Curtis Yarvin, a software developer and techno-libertarian political writer who publishes under the name Mencius Moldbug, argued that much of the fear around AI resembles a modern "golem" myth. He saw that fear as more myth than realistic forecast. His point was that intelligence by itself does not become power, since machines still operate through institutions, owners, incentives, and commands. That shifts the focus away from the machine as a monster and toward the people and organizations deciding where the machine gets used.

Yarvin's argument is useful even for readers who reject his politics because it shows how much ideology shapes the AI debate. For some people, AI risk is mainly a labor story about displacement, inequality, and declining security. For others, panic about AI inflates the technology and the status of the people building it. The disagreement is partly technical, but it is also a fight over power. Different groups are arguing about whose fear counts as realistic and whose fear gets dismissed as hysteria.

What ties these voices together is the sense that the old rules are under pressure. Dimon imagines AI as a productivity engine, Grimes speaks about abundance, Amodei warns that the builders may be moving faster than society can absorb, and Yang asks who should pay for the disruption. Yarvin adds a different question by doubting that technology itself is the real center of power. The disagreement is about who controls the transition and who is left exposed by it. Occupational risk makes that argument more concrete because it moves the debate away from speculation and toward a more specific labor story.

Any occupational-risk model simplifies reality, but the pattern is still useful. The model points to where exposure is likely to concentrate first, and that concentration matters because the pressure will land unevenly across the labor market.

That unevenness becomes clearer at the level of actual jobs. Public conversation often treats automation as if it will sweep across the whole economy at once. In practice, AI pressure is more likely to follow places where employers already have reasons to cut labor costs, monitor performance, and standardize tasks. Broad claims about the future of work can hide the people who feel that pressure first. A national productivity gain can still arrive as a local layoff, a reduced schedule, or a new software system that makes a job faster and less humane.

Broken out by sector, the hierarchy becomes hard to miss. Service work appears especially exposed, with most of those jobs falling into the high-risk category, while skilled trades also show substantial vulnerability. Healthcare and education remain more insulated because so much of their work depends on judgment, care, trust, and responsibility. Business and legal work sit between those poles. Risk follows organizational power as much as technical possibility. The sectors with the weakest ability to resist efficiency demands are often the same sectors where employers can describe work in clean units: calls answered, packages moved, shifts filled, transactions completed, tasks closed.

The most vulnerable work is usually built around repetition, routines, and tasks that can be divided into predictable steps. Safer work often depends on expertise, authority, care, or forms of interaction that remain difficult to standardize at scale. That does not make those jobs permanently safe, but it does make them less likely to be the first target. Timing matters because workers hit first often have the least room to prepare. A worker who loses hours before a retraining program exists is living through the gap between fast corporate adoption and slower public support.

Public anxiety around AI becomes concrete once the discussion moves from broad sectors to actual jobs. A cashier, customer-support worker, or logistics employee is not in the same position as a doctor, teacher, or lawyer, even if all of them may eventually use AI tools. The difference lies in how easily a role can be turned into a system that managers can monitor, standardize, and scale. Once that conversion becomes possible, economic pressure gives employers a reason to try it. The worker may still be present, but the job can become more scripted, more closely watched, and more tightly measured until the human part of the work is treated as secondary.

AI is reaching parts of the labor market that were already exposed before generative AI became familiar to everyone. The highest-risk jobs often come with less security, weaker bargaining power, lower status, and less control over schedules or working conditions. These are also jobs where managers have long talked about labor through numbers: calls handled, shifts filled, quotas met, time saved. AI gives companies another way to make cuts they were already considering. A cashier or support worker may hear an executive talk about innovation and hear something more immediate: fewer hours, tighter scripts, more monitoring, or a manager expecting the same staff to cover more ground.

That history explains why the debate feels so charged. Automation is arriving in a labor market that was already unequal, and many workers suspect the new technology will deepen those inequalities unless policy interrupts the pattern. That suspicion comes from experience with earlier waves of innovation, which raised productivity while leaving many households with less security than the broad numbers implied. The real test is whether the people exposed to innovation's costs have any claim on its rewards.

From the top of the economic ladder, AI can look exciting, efficient, and even freeing. From the edge of the labor market, it can look like another force pushing people toward replacement or downward mobility. The same technology produces promises of leisure in one corner and fears of losing ground in another because people are looking at it from different positions. The debate often sounds confused because participants are not imagining the same economy.

What looks like optimism or fear often depends on how much room a person has to absorb change. Savings, credentials, professional networks, and authority at work all shape how someone experiences technological disruption. A worker with a financial cushion can treat reskilling as a challenge. A worker living paycheck to paycheck may experience the same transition as a threat to rent, health care, or family stability.

The same split becomes more complicated inside tech itself. Technology work is often treated as the safest corner of the economy, but the role-level pattern is more uneven. AI engineers and research scientists appear strongly protected because they help design, evaluate, or direct the systems reshaping other work. Software engineers, data scientists, graphic designers, and UX researchers are still far from the service-worker risk category, though they are not in the same position as the roles closest to AI infrastructure. Technical skill matters, but it does not create equal security by itself.

That split makes the word "tech" too broad to explain what is happening. Some workers are treated as architects of the transition, while others are close enough to automation that parts of their labor can be absorbed into tools, workflows, or smaller teams. The first sign may be acceleration rather than unemployment: fewer entry-level openings, more output expected from smaller teams, and less patience for the slower learning curve that used to train new workers.

The difference may look subtle now, but it can become large over time. Someone building AI systems has a different position from someone whose design, coding, or analytical work is increasingly supported by those systems. Salary and education matter, but the structure of the task may matter more when employers decide where automation can save money. Jobs built around procedure and repetition are more exposed than jobs built around judgment, creativity, expertise, or trust.

The tech split points back to a larger question of status. Retail workers, security guards, construction workers, customer-support workers, and truck drivers do different jobs. Still, much of their work can be described through routines, task boundaries, and outputs that companies can imagine cutting, outsourcing, or partially automating. AI inherits that hierarchy instead of creating it from scratch, building on work arrangements that were already easier to monitor and cheaper to pressure.

The same technology can therefore feel very different across the labor market. A professional worker may experience AI as software that removes a tedious task. A lower-wage worker may experience it as the reason a supervisor cuts a shift, raises a quota, or decides that a smaller staff can handle the same workload. The difference is technical, but it is also about who has enough security to treat change as manageable and who has to absorb it as another demand from above.

At the other end of the spectrum are jobs such as doctor, nurse, teacher, AI engineer, and research scientist. These roles depend heavily on judgment, care, expertise, trust, or public responsibility. AI may still alter diagnosis, lesson planning, research workflows, and administrative work, but these roles are farther from the immediate pressure to replace workers with systems.

The distance between those protected roles and more exposed jobs turns the technology story into a political one. Automation is entering a labor market that was already highly unequal, and the same patterns of exposure could deepen those divisions unless policy and worker protections interrupt them. The jobs most exposed to automation are often the jobs least able to demand protection on their own. Who bears the risk will depend on unions, regulation, public investment, employer choices, and the willingness to treat some workers as worth protecting.

When a category of work is displaced or downgraded, the damage is financial and social at the same time. A layoff, wage cut, or reduction in hours also carries a judgment about whose labor is considered irreplaceable and whose work can be cut away. Employment remains one of the main ways people prove usefulness, stability, and belonging. When work loses value, the consequences spread into family life, local economies, mental health, and political trust.

Automation debates keep drifting into moral language because they are arguments about obligation as much as efficiency. If society benefits from labor-saving technology, it has to decide what it owes the people whose labor becomes less necessary. Minimal support turns innovation into abandonment for those who absorb the shock. Stronger wages, benefits, training, bargaining power, and redistribution can make the same technology produce a different outcome. The moral question is also practical because workers who experience change as abandonment are less likely to trust the institutions asking them to adapt.

The broader economic history sharpens the point. Decades earlier, Robert Solow, the Nobel Prize-winning economist associated with the study of economic growth, observed that the computer age seemed to appear everywhere except in the productivity statistics. The paradox was eventually resolved when productivity surged in the late 1990s, but the gains did not arrive evenly. Wages for many workers at the bottom and middle moved slowly while returns at the top accelerated. The lesson for the AI era is direct: productivity growth and shared prosperity are separate outcomes. That history should make leaders cautious when they promise that a more efficient economy will automatically become a fairer one. Fair distribution has to be built; it does not appear on its own.

The most plausible danger is probably not a sudden collapse of the whole labor market. A more likely outcome is a sorting process that rewards workers whose skills complement machines while pressuring workers whose jobs can be described as a sequence of steps. AI can create value without creating broadly shared value, raising efficiency without making households more secure. That distinction matters more than dramatic predictions about total job loss.

Policy has to begin before displacement becomes a crisis. If the pressure falls hardest on service work, logistics, and the trades, stronger labor protections, wage insurance, portable benefits, better training systems, public investment, and redistribution all belong in the conversation. Yang's warning is useful if it forces policy makers to plan before the shock arrives. Dimon's optimism becomes realistic only if societies deliberately share the gains instead of assuming they will spread on their own. A serious response would also measure job quality, because a labor market can preserve employment while degrading schedules, wages, autonomy, and the predictability people need to build stable lives.

That requires a difficult admission: technology does not decide the character of the change by itself. Institutions, markets, management decisions, and public policy turn technical ability into lived reality. Bargaining power matters because workers with leverage can shape how tools are adopted. Workers without leverage often experience the same tools as commands from above. If AI widens inequality, the cause will include the technology, but it will also include the weakness of the response around it.

No occupational model can predict the future with certainty. Transitions are uneven, and labor markets are too complex for that kind of precision. Still, the direction of pressure is visible. Some workers will benefit from AI early, especially those whose judgment and authority are strengthened by the tools. Others will absorb disruption through reduced hours, tighter monitoring, lower bargaining power, or displacement. The central question is who pays for that change, and whether the gains are shared widely enough to make the transition defensible. The answer will come from political choices made while the technology is still new enough for its rules to be contested.
